00

The queue, not the build, is where the time goes.

This section explains: the whole argument in five sentences, for a reader who stops here.

One-minute version

A pull request on this team turns green about 25 minutes after it opens, and that gap has roughly doubled since the start of the quarter.

The gap contains two intervals that respond to opposite treatments, so it was measured per job rather than per workflow.

The median splits into 19.8 minutes of waiting for a free machine and 4.8 minutes of actually building.

An earlier comparison that ranked rented machines ahead of the team's own turned out to compare two different sets of jobs; holding the job type fixed reverses the order.

01

Waiting for CI is two waits stacked, and each one has the opposite cure.

This section explains: why a single "CI is slow" number cannot be acted on, and what the two parts of it are.

A developer opens a pull request and watches a status check. Some minutes later the check turns green. The gap between those two moments is what the developer feels, and this page calls it the felt wait.

The felt wait contains two intervals. In the first, called queue wait, the job exists and no machine has picked it up yet. In the second, called build time, a machine is running the steps written in the workflow file.

The machines come from one of two places. A self-hosted runner pool is a fixed set of machines the team owns and pays for whether or not a job is running, so a job waits when all of them are busy. A managed runner is rented from the CI provider for the length of one job, so a job never waits for a free machine and every minute of build time appears on the bill.

The two intervals respond to opposite treatments. Queue wait shrinks when more machines exist or fewer jobs compete for them. Build time shrinks when the steps do less work: a warmer cache, a smaller image, a suite split across shards. Neither treatment reaches the other interval.

Here is the problem: one averaged number hides which of the two intervals grew. Buying machines to fix a slow build costs money and changes nothing. Caching harder to fix a queue removes minutes that were never there.
Colours, used the same way in all three figures: grey is queue wait, the interval where nothing runs. Orange is build time, the interval under discussion. Green marks the outcome worth having. Red marks a number or a comparison that cannot be trusted. Blue is the direction of flow.
02

Separating the two waits needed three timestamps per job, not one stopwatch.

This section explains: what was recorded, how the two intervals were derived from it, and what the sample covers.

The duration shown next to a check on the pull request page starts counting when the job is created, which is before any machine has accepted it. That single number therefore already contains the queue wait, and reading it as build time is the mistake section 03 withdraws.

Each job record carries three timestamps instead: the moment the job was created, the moment a runner accepted it, and the moment it finished. Queue wait is the first gap and build time is the second. The sample is 4,100 job runs across 11 repositories over three weeks, weekdays only, with cancelled runs dropped.

03

The first reading blamed the build steps, and re-measuring moved four fifths of the wait into the queue.

This section explains: which earlier conclusion is withdrawn, why it was wrong, and which of the numbers are safe to repeat out loud.

Correction

What was said before: the slowdown came from the test steps, because a lockfile update had pulled in a heavier dependency tree, and the fix was to shard the suite three ways.

Why that was wrong: the duration read off the pull request page starts at job creation, so it already contained the queue wait. A longer total was read as a longer build. No step-level timing was opened at all.

What to believe now: per-job timestamps put 19.8 of the 24.6 median minutes in the queue. The step timings did rise, from 3.6 to 4.8 median minutes, which is real and accounts for under a tenth of the 13.4-minute growth in felt wait.

Sharding the suite would have been worth roughly one minute of the thirteen. The work was already scoped for two engineers and a week, which is the cost of acting on the wrong interval.

04

The comparison that made rented machines look faster was comparing two different sets of jobs.

This section explains: how a difference in job mix produced a ranking, and what that ranking becomes once the mix is held fixed.

A trial in the second week sent part of the traffic to managed runners. Pooled across everything that ran, a job finished in 5.1 minutes on the rented machines against 7.4 minutes on the pool. Read on its own, that gap says rent more and own less.

The gap is confounded by job mix, meaning which kinds of job each side happened to run. The integration suite needs a licensed database container that is pinned to the pool machines, so it never entered the managed lane. Heavy jobs were 31 percent of the pool lane and 4 percent of the managed lane.

LaneHeavy jobsMean, all jobsMean, unit tests only
Self-hosted pool31%7.4 min4.1 min
Managed runners4%5.1 min5.8 min

Both readings survive together. Rented machines remove the queue, and they run each individual job more slowly. That is exactly why the answer in section 05 is a split rather than a winner.

05

On like-for-like numbers the split lane ranks first, and the reason it ranks first can be checked.

This section explains: the three options, the number each ranking turns on, and the observation that would overturn it.

Every felt-wait figure in these three cards is modelled from the arrival counts in the sample, not observed after a change. They are labelled as estimates for that reason. The cost figures are list price with no negotiated discount applied.

The queue forms for about six hours on a weekday and is empty the rest of the time. It removes most of the queue and changes nothing about how jobs are written, which is worth real money in avoided confusion. 06

Three weeks of weekdays leaves three gaps, and each one changes a specific choice.

This section explains: what the sample cannot support, and which follow-up closes each gap.

Count the queue after routing, not before
The 9.4-minute figure for option 1 is modelled from arrival counts and has never been observed. Two weeks of the same three timestamps, taken after the routing rule ships, settles it. Decision 2 turns on this number, which is why decision 2 should wait for it.
Start the heavy job on a rented machine once
The licensed database container has never run outside the pool. A single integration run on a rented machine says whether option 3 is available at all, and it costs an afternoon.
Re-measure inside a release week
The sample contains no release freeze, and freezes are when concurrent demand peaks. Until one is measured, treat 41 concurrent jobs as a floor rather than a ceiling, and size any pool change against that reading.

Until the first two are done, the platform update should carry the split and the correction and no cost figure. A cost number quoted now would be an estimate reprinted as a measurement, which is how the 18.4-minute figure got into circulation in the first place.

07

Three answers are needed, and the first one blocks the other two.

Decision 1 sets the direction for every repository; decisions 2 and 3 only affect spending and what gets said in public.

Decision 1 · affects every repository

Which lane does the default runner label point at?

Today one label points at the pool and every job inherits it. The split option needs two labels and a rule that decides between them. The cost of getting it wrong is a week of confused pull requests, because a mislabelled job silently lands in the slower lane.

My recommendation: keep the default on the pool and move only named heavy jobs plus queue overflow to rented machines. The like-for-like numbers in section 04 say the pool runs an ordinary job faster, so the default should stay where the cache already is. The overflow rule is what removes the queue.
Decision 2 · affects the quarter's budget

Does the pool grow now, or after the routing rule has run for two weeks?

Eight more machines cost 67 percent more per month and would remove the queue on their own. Ordering them after the split ships risks discovering that the queue is already gone and the machines are not needed.

My recommendation: wait. Every post-routing wait figure on this page is modelled rather than observed, and the follow-up in section 06 replaces it with a measurement in two weeks. Spending 67 percent more to avoid a two-week wait is the wrong trade unless the release freeze lands inside those two weeks.
Decision 3 · affects what leaves the team

Does the sentence "managed runners are faster" stay in the platform update?

It came from the pooled comparison in section 04, where the two lanes were running different jobs. Once the sentence is outside the team it will be quoted back during the next tooling argument, and the mix caveat will not travel with it.

My recommendation: delete the sentence. Replace it with the finding that queue wait is 80 percent of the median, which is the part that holds up and the part that explains what changes next.
